
## **Two piles, one shelf**
We can think of this collection as essentially:


**Great Books Pile** Syllabus / prestige — books people put on lists and assign in school. Shakespeare, Austen, Tolstoy, Walden, Marcus Aurelius, Britannica Great Books, Guardian/BBC/Modern Library titles. Some of that is dense (philosophy, Conrad). A lot of it isn’t (Pride and Prejudice is not pulp, and it isn’t a slog either).

**Old (pulp) Fiction** Pastime fiction that aged into the public domain — what people actually bought to be entertained, 1880–1930 or so. Mystery, adventure, early SF, comic novels, series. Holmes, Christie, Verne, Wells, Wodehouse, Tarzan, Hardy Boys, Scarlet Pimpernel. Pulp is the loudest corner of this pile, not the whole pile. Wodehouse is light, not pulp. Trollope is Victorian social novel, not Burroughs.



The bulk of this library is English-language public-domain literature, produced like a small press rather than a dump of scans. Semantically it is two piles sharing one shelf.

About 60% is fiction, and a lot of that is not “Great Books.” It is Victorian–Edwardian–interwar entertainment: mystery, adventure, early science fiction, comedy, and pulp. The densest authors are Shakespeare, then Wodehouse, Burroughs, Trollope, Conrad, Edgar Wallace, Verne, Shaw, Balzac, Dickens. That mix is the tell. This is a pre-1929 (plus some later PD) anglophone warehouse: drawing-room novels, detective series, planetary romance, Hardy Boys, Holmes, Tarzan, Scarlet Pimpernel — plus a real canon layer (Britannica Great Books, Guardian/BBC/Modern Library lists, 86 philosophy titles, Shakespeare, Tolstoy, Austen).

It is not a general bookstore. There is no Romance subject. Biography is almost empty (7). Children’s exists (~84) but many of the books people mean by “kids’ classics” are still in copyright (Narnia is on their collection pages, not in the downloadable catalog). Nonfiction-ish tags cover only ~230 books. Everything is English editions. The constraint is copyright and editorial taste, not “what readers want in 2026.”

Who would feel it is satisfying

People like Mia: they want Pride and Prejudice, Walden, Holmes, Dickens, and a file that does not look like Project Gutenberg’s worst HTML. They already believe classics should be free to own.
Canon chasers — Great Books, undergrad philosophy, “I should read the Russians / the Greeks / Shakespeare.”
Vintage genre readers who actually like Christie, Doyle, Wells, Verne, Wodehouse, Burroughs, and golden-age mystery more than they like Booker-list 2024.
Anyone who wants a finite, non-algorithmic library: no “you might also like,” no Kindle Unlimited slurry, just a curated PD corpus with good typography.
Who would be underwhelmed

Readers whose “classics” come from current BookTok/romantasy (Yao, Maas, Hoover, backlist that’s still in copyright). They will see 1,500 titles and still not find the book they meant.
People hunting living authors, recent prizes, or a complete 20th-century syllabus (Morrison, Baldwin’s later work, Woolf is patchy by PD, LOTR/Narnia/Potter, contemporary literary fiction).
Romance, YA, comics/manga, and “something new this week” shoppers. The catalog does not pretend to be that.
Readers who want global, contemporary, or systematically diverse shelves. Coverage of women, non-Western, and 20th-century marginalized writers is whatever happened to fall into U.S. public domain and get produced — not a collection-development policy.
Specialists who need critical editions, facing-page originals, or completeness of a national literature. This is a reading library, not a research library.
One-line read: it will feel rich to someone whose taste is “dead authors, good pages, I own the file.” It will feel oddly both stuffy and pulpy — and still incomplete — to someone whose taste was formed by a modern bookstore or by BookTok that isn’t specifically classic BookTok.

Who would feel well served
- Stoicism-and-ancients people. Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, Seneca, Cicero, plus Plato’s Dialogues, Aristotle’s Ethics, Boethius, Augustine. That cluster is the most coherent “I want to be a serious person” starter pack on the whole SE catalog.
- Undergrad survey readers doing Locke, Hume, Hobbes, Rousseau, Mill, Descartes, Smith, Machiavelli. Enough to walk a standard “history of Western political thought / early modern” course without owning a bookstore.
- Political and moral essayists. Paine, Burke, Wollstonecraft, Marx, Mill, Dewey, James, Du Bois, Cooper, Gilman, the anarchists (Kropotkin, Proudhon, Spooner). This is actually a feature: SE’s “Philosophy” tag is half social thought.
- Pragmatism / progressive-era America. James and Dewey are unusually well represented for a PD catalog.
- Chesterton / Tolstoy / Thoreau / Emerson types — philosophy as literature and temperament, not as papers.

Who would bounce off it
- Anyone whose mental map is Kant → Hegel → Nietzsche as a system. Nietzsche is here; Kant and Hegel basically aren’t. That’s a hole you can drive a German department through.
- Specialists. One Dialogues for Plato, Ethics only for Aristotle, no Aquinas, no Spinoza/Leibniz/Berkeley as a set, no Kierkegaard. It is editions they happened to produce, not a corpus.
- Continental 20th century (Husserl, Heidegger, Sartre, Camus, Arendt’s later work) and analytic after the Tractatus (Russell here is the popular Russell, not Principia). Wittgenstein’s Investigations, Quine, Rawls: no.
- A serious Chinese / Indian / Islamic philosophy reader. Analects, Tao Te Ching, and The Kural are gestures, not a tradition.

Rough satisfaction
- Stoic / “read the classics on my phone” reader: ~70–80%
- Political-theory amateur (liberalism, socialism, anarchism): ~60–70%
- Philosophy major who wants the real historical spine, including German idealism and 20th century: ~30–40%
- Graduate or specialist: underwhelmed, full stop

One-line version: this collection satisfies the person who wants Marcus Aurelius and Mill on the train, not the person who wants Kant on the desk.
