# **What is a persona, and why do we have them?**
When we design products, we want to build them with real human users in mind. Of course, our process involves talking to real users and hearing about their needs and preferences. However, we don't like to refer to real humans—that brings up privacy issues, and also risks that we'll over-fit an app for one individual. 

That is why we create personas. Personas are "who" the team builds for. They're fake people that we construct based on conversations with real people. 

Our team has two personas:

1. **Gretta, the Great Books Reader** *Gretta has set a goal for herself to read the great books. She is an avid reader of day to day pop fiction, but has fallen into the idea that she wants to read harder books and the classics. 

2. **Persona: Aaron - the anarchy-leaning political-philosophy reader.** a politically curious autodidact who would be happy in a used bookstore’s “social questions” section. He thinks deep thoughts about political philosophy, and likes the idea that the state or large corporations aren't intermediating his reading. 


Over time, we will refine these personas or add more—but Gretta and Aaron are our starting place. 

## **How we constructed these personas**
Personas are a perpetual work-in-progress. They are born of hypotheses, and refined as we talk to real people and understand their needs at a deeper level.

### **Step 1: We analyzed the Standard Ebooks Catalogue**
We know that the Standard Ebooks catalogue will be our first "library" that we're offer to users, so we want to scope our target users to the people that will be well-served by that library. This is what we found:
- **Great Books.** Prestige books people put on lists and assign in school. Shakespeare, Austen, Tolstoy, Walden, Marcus Aurelius, Britannica Great Books, Guardian/BBC/Modern Library titles. Some of that is dense (philosophy, Conrad). A lot of it isn’t (Pride and Prejudice is not pulp, and it isn’t a slog either).
- **Old Popular Shelf.** Pastime fiction that aged into the public domain — what people actually bought to be entertained, 1880–1930 or so. Mystery, adventure, early SF, comic novels, series. Holmes, Christie, Verne, Wells, Wodehouse, Tarzan, Hardy Boys, Scarlet Pimpernel. Pulp is the loudest corner of this pile, not the whole pile. Wodehouse is light, not pulp. Trollope is Victorian social novel, not Burroughs.
- **Political Philosophy Readers.** A lot of books that are interesting to readers of political philosophy, particularly liberal/anarchis philosophy. Names like Kropotkin, Proudhon, Goldman, Russel, Tolstoy, Hobbes, Machiavelli and more.
