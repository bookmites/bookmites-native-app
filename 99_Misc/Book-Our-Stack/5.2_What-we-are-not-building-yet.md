## Chapter 5.2 — What we are not building yet (honest boundary)

### Key vocabulary

**BYOK**, short for "bring your own key," is an approach to AI features in which the user supplies their own account key for an AI provider, so the app does not pay for or broker the service. A **companion file** is an auxiliary file attached to a book, such as notes or a guide, that a future feature could use alongside the epub. **EAS**, Expo Application Services, is Expo's cloud service for building and distributing apps, first mentioned in Chapter 2.1. **Dedupe** is short for deduplication, the detection of a file the library already holds. **Parity** means that two platforms offer the same features and quality.

### Why a chapter about absence

A book about a technology stack tends to leave readers with the impression that everything it mentions is already built. That would be a mistake here. The earlier chapters describe the decisions that shape the first phase of the Reader, a local epub reader on Android, and they sometimes point beyond it: a hook for AI in the adapter, a mention of backup beside the storage layers, a note that deduplication is undecided. A reader who comes across these and then searches the book for the chapter that explains them will not find one. This chapter exists so that the search can end quickly. It lists what is deliberately left out, gives enough context to understand why the stack looks the way it does with those items in mind, and says plainly that none of them is required for the central goal, which is to read a book well. The architecture document puts it in one sentence: none of these block the phase in which a reader can open a book and read it.

This chapter does not restate the product's plans. It is concerned with what each omission means for the machinery described in this book.

### In-app AI and BYOK

The first omission is the AI features. The adapter in Chapter 3.3 mentions a future operation that returns the text around a location, and the data model in Chapter 4.1 mentions future chats among the user artifacts. Neither exists yet. No AI provider is called, no chat is stored, and no screen for entering a key is built.

The BYOK approach affects the stack in a few predictable ways when it arrives. The key the user provides is a secret, and secrets should not sit in the ordinary database beside highlights. They belong in the phone's secure storage, which Expo exposes through a dedicated module. The requests to the AI provider will go from the phone directly, which fits the product's local-first and private stance, because the Bookmites organization does not stand in the middle. And the passages the AI sees will come from the adapter's text-around-a-location operation, which is why that verb was planned into the adapter's design early, even though nobody calls it today.

What matters for this book is the ordering. An AI feature that discusses a passage is only as good as the reader's ability to identify that passage reliably, and that depends on locators, on the adapter, and on the book opening correctly. These are the subject of Part III, and they are the foundation the first phase is meant to make solid.

### Companion files

The second omission is the companion file. The idea is that a book could have something attached to it, such as a set of notes, a summary, or a guide, that travels with the book and informs later features. The architecture document lists these among user artifacts that will come later, along with chats.

Nothing in the first phase implements them, but the data model has been shaped to leave room. Because artifacts are linked to a book by its `bookId` and not by its file, a companion file would be one more kind of artifact in the same relationship, living in the app's private storage or in the database as the design requires. The choice of where it would live has not been made, and this book does not make it.

### Cloud backup

The third omission is backup. The product intends to let users copy their data to the cloud as an option, but the first phase assumes that the phone is the only home for everything. A reader's highlights and positions live in SQLite on the device, and the epub copies live in private storage, as Part IV described. If the phone is lost, so is the data.

Two features of the current design prepare for backup without implementing it. The first is that all user data is concentrated in two known places, the database and the files, so a future export has a clear target. The second is that the artifacts are stored in plain forms, a locator as a JSON string and a highlight as a row, which can be written out and read back without translation. What remains undecided is the destination, whether that is a user's own drive account or another service, and how conflicts between devices are resolved. These are substantial questions, and the book leaves them alone.

### Store release and signing

The fourth omission is publication to a store. Chapter 2.2 explained that debug builds are signed automatically with a generic key, and that a release build should be signed with a keystore the project owns. In the first phase, builds go to the team's own phones, and to early testers by sideloading, so the generic key is sufficient.

Releasing on the Play Store will require much more than a key. It means a stable signing arrangement that will last for the life of the app, store listings, a review of the target SDK version against the store's requirements, and release builds that have been tested on a range of real devices. EAS may become useful here, since it can run builds in the cloud and manage credentials, but this is an option to be taken up when the time comes, and not a part of daily work. A reader should treat the signing material as something to introduce deliberately, once, and not as something to improvise near a deadline.

### iOS parity

The fifth omission is the iPhone. The architecture document states that the first phase is Android-first for builds and testing, with iOS as part of the wider phase but not part of the initial integration work. The choice of React Native and Expo keeps this door open, since most of the code is shared, and Readium supports both platforms through the same library.

This is not the same as saying iOS will work without effort. The native layer differs, with its own build tooling, its own file-access rules, and its own way of rendering text. Chapter 2.2 is about Android for this reason, and the physical-device testing described throughout this book would have to be repeated on an iPhone. Parity is a goal, and it is not an achievement of the current phase.

### Deduplication by file hash

The last omission is a small one, and a good example of an honest gap. If a reader imports the same epub twice, the current design will make two copies and two book records, because nothing checks whether the file is already in the library. The architecture document records this as undecided and notes that deduplication by file hash is to be considered later.

A hash is a short fingerprint computed from a file's contents, so two identical files produce the same fingerprint. Comparing fingerprints at import would identify duplicates reliably, even if the files had different names. The same fingerprint could also help address the edition problem raised in Chapter 3.4, in which a locator saved against one version of a book is applied to a different version. The cost is complexity: the import flow gets another step, and there are questions about what to do when a duplicate is found, such as whether to refuse, merge, or ask. The current phase accepts the extra disk space as a small price.

### Common pitfalls and gotchas

The central pitfall of this chapter is building the later features before the foundation is solid. An AI chat is attractive and visible, and it is tempting to start on it early. But it depends on stable locators, on a dependable adapter, and on a reading experience that people trust, and an AI feature layered on a position system that occasionally loses its place will inherit that unreliability and amplify it. The order of work in the outline of this book, which moves from the engine to the adapter to the locators, is also the order in which these features become safe to attempt.

A second pitfall is designing for the future so thoroughly that the present suffers. The hooks mentioned in this book, such as the text-around-a-location operation, are small and cost little. A sprawling set of empty tables, unused settings, and half-built abstractions for features that may change before they are built is a cost the project pays on every change. The sensible rule is to leave room, as the data model does with its `bookId` link, and not to build the contents of the room.

A third is mistaking silence for decision. Several items in this chapter are marked as undecided in the project's documents: where backup goes, how duplicates are handled, whether companion files live in the filesystem or the database. When a team is busy, an undecided question can quietly become settled by whatever the first implementation happens to do. Writing the question down, as this chapter does, makes it harder for that to happen without anyone noticing.

The last pitfall is reading a boundary as a flaw. A reader who notices that the app cannot yet back up, chat, or run on an iPhone may wonder whether the stack is incomplete. It is not. It is sized to the work in front of it, and the reasoning behind each choice in this book was to make that work succeed first, while keeping the later work possible.
